# Series 01 — Muslim female protagonist development

Established at planning exchange 18. Discovery and research notes, not a completed character brief.

## Director direction

- Strong Muslim female lead is established in the series premise.
- Director wants specific community/religious grounding for all protagonists, with prominence in the story to be decided later; the purpose is richness and authenticity rather than vague South Asian caricature.
- Muslim Pashtun identity accepted in exchange 19: Director says “ok let's go with muslim pashtun”. Specific region, community and household remain open.
- Director envisions her as beautiful, strong, strongheaded and a warrior working alongside men. These qualities do not establish a sexualized presentation, emotional invulnerability, contempt for other women or rejection of her faith.
- Region, family/community ties, age, training, practical freedoms, personal aims and reasons for working with the officer remain open. Do not invent a rescue by the British, forced-marriage origin or family violence by default.

## Research and recommendation — exchange 18

Recommendation: do not abandon a Pashtun character to accommodate Taliban restrictions or anticipated disapproval. Care about historical credibility, nuanced representation, contributor safety and informed critique rather than treating any one group's objection as a veto. Audience reaction cannot be predicted or quantified from this quick research.

Current context: [UN Women, March 2026 briefing](https://www.unwomen.org/en/news-stories/press-briefing/2026/03/press-briefing-by-un-women-on-afghanistan) describes continuing restrictions and the danger of their normalization. Search-indexed institutional text was available; direct page retrieval failed. This supports the present-day rights concern, not a claim that Taliban rules describe all Pashtuns, Islam, Afghan women or nineteenth-century life.

Historical lead: Sameetah Agha, [Women and Warfare in British India's North-West Frontier](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/63111/chapter-abstract/567592947), in *The Oxford Handbook of Warfare in Asia* (2026), pp. 207–222, DOI 10.1093/9780198969631.003.0013. The publisher's indexed abstract identifies women's active and sometimes direct roles in frontier warfare through colonial-source clues and Pakhtun oral traditions. **Only the abstract was reviewed**, not the full chapter. This challenges an assumption of total female absence; it does not qualify our exact mixed-gender covert team, training history or employment under a British officer.

Jatt Sikh, Hindu Gurkha and Pashtun Muslim are useful specific starting points, but not equivalent category types or complete biographies. Community, locality, class, kinship, language and personal practice still matter. Pashtun identity should not be collapsed into Taliban affiliation or present-day Afghan citizenship. Avoid projecting current borders and political institutions backward.

## Development requirements proposed for her brief

1. Ground her skills in a credible, researched route to training and experience; specify strengths and limits rather than granting universal action-hero competence.
2. Make her participation possible through particular relationships and circumstances, with varied reactions—not universal welcome or universal hostility.
3. Give her her own reason to cooperate with the officer and meaningful limits on that cooperation. Her agency must not be bestowed by a supposedly civilizing British rescuer.
4. Allow Muslim faith, beauty, feminine expression and martial skill to coexist in ways she chooses. Do not describe courage as inherently male or require emancipation through rejection of culture.
5. Include women and men from her world as distinct people; do not make her the only worthwhile person from a uniformly oppressive community.
6. Seek paid historical/cultural review from relevant Pashtun women and a period specialist before script lock, within an approved budget. No outreach or spending authorized. No individual reviewer represents every Pashtun perspective; contributor credit/anonymity should be consensual.

A promising dramatic question, not canon: what objective is important enough that she accepts an alliance with this British officer while retaining her own loyalties? This may require at least as much explanation as her combat skills. No backstory, ethnic sub-group, historical military role or episode plot selected yet.

## Recruitment geography — exchange 19

Director asks whether British Pathan recruits came from what is now Pakistan/Khyber Pakhtunkhwa rather than Afghanistan. Findings:

- “Pathan” is the Indo-Aryan/Hindustani designation used extensively in British sources for Pashtuns; Pashtun/Pakhtun reflect linguistic variants, not separate modern national populations. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Pashto](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/afghanistan-vi-pasto/) discusses the term's linguistic derivation; [Library of Congress catalogue](https://www.loc.gov/item/02013572) identifies the terminology in a colonial tribal dictionary. Do not call Pathan exclusively Pakistani and Pashtun exclusively Afghan.
- Recruitment from the frontier is established before our candidate date: the [National Army Museum overview](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/north-west-frontier-india) includes Pathan soldiers of the 20th Punjab Native Infantry in 1868. It distinguishes directly administered districts from largely autonomous adjacent territory; colonial classifications and rhetoric need critical handling.
- Much of the relevant geography is in today's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. In the 1870s–1880s, administered frontier districts belonged to Punjab; North-West Frontier Province was created in 1901. Not every territory in the modern province shared the same nineteenth-century status. The [1893 Durand agreement](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/boundaries-iii/) postdates our candidate setting; an earlier frontier existed but was not today's national border. Do not place modern citizenship categories or a province called Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in period dialogue.
- A [Royal Historical Society research essay on 1919](https://blog.royalhistsoc.org/2019/05/06/third-anglo-afghan-war/) describes recruitment from northwestern India and Afghanistan. Its indexed text was available; direct retrieved text did not expose the article body. This supports caution about categorically excluding Afghan-origin recruits, but does not establish a particular 1870s Afghan-subject recruitment channel. Cross-frontier origin must not automatically be equated with Afghan state jurisdiction. Exact unit/date/origin evidence remains necessary.

Working regional direction accepted at exchange 20: investigate a Pashtun household in the Peshawar–Mardan area first. This does not fix a birthplace, tribe or household. Possible family service or intermediary connections are fictional leads, not established facts. Her own reason to cooperate and training must still be developed independently. **Evidence of male recruitment does not establish routine female enlistment; the fictional team need not have an exact historical counterpart.**

Keep three questions separate: where she is from; how contact with the officer arose; what she personally wants from the alliance. Choose her region and date before attaching a named tribe or real unit to her family.

## Fiction standard and next creative move — exchange 20

Director explicitly confirms historically inspired fiction, not a documentary. Invent a credible personal path rather than seeking permission from a documented identical woman. Preserve research boundaries for real-history claims without making every invented detail a new research gate.

Proposal for discussion, not accepted: her alliance may give her information and leverage over events affecting people or places she cares about. She is not seeking British recognition of her worth; she wants a practical say in outcomes that would otherwise be decided elsewhere. The officer needs her judgment and connections, while she needs access he can offer. This can be a negotiated arrangement even though the officer's accepted relationship with the Sikh protagonist is not consciously transactional. Do not automatically infer a threatened village, murdered family, espionage training or disloyalty to either side. Ask whether the Director wants this direction or has a different personal ambition in mind.

## Protection and ambition — exchange 21, exploratory

Director chooses to explore the intersection of protecting something and personal ambition. This accepts an exploration direction, not a specific external aim, biography or flaw; brief remains 1/6 complete.

Three fictional possibilities for discussion (not historical claims or canon):

1. Protect a family livelihood while building something of her own: for example, a household connected to horses, transport or trade. She wants security and also expansion, ownership and achievement. The alliance might create access but expose the livelihood to imperial dependence. Specific occupation and period mechanisms require later grounding if selected.
2. Protect people she belongs to by becoming someone whose judgment carries weight. She wants to affect decisions before damage is done, and genuinely enjoys responsibility, strategy and being listened to. The officer offers access, not authority to represent her people. Conflict: securing a compromise may advance her standing while leaving some of those people worse off; they can legitimately reject her choices.
3. Protect a particular person while pursuing a life larger than that obligation. An initially bounded protective bargain opens opportunities she wants to keep after the original danger passes. Avoid defaulting to a hostage relative or permanent victim-rescuer structure.

Recommendation for discussion: option 2, with a concrete personal/family anchor to be developed. Possible dramatic question: when does protecting people become deciding for them? This is not an accepted controlling trait. Her ambition should remain a real source of pleasure and capability, not be disguised entirely as self-sacrifice or punished merely because she wants power. Distinguish her possible representational dilemma from the Sikh's accepted difficulty admitting uncertainty and the Gurkha's difficulty letting others manage without him.

## Accepted motivational direction — exchange 22

Director selects option 2 anchored in something concrete and intimate from option 1. Her external aim is to protect people she belongs to by becoming someone whose judgment carries weight, rooted in particular personal ties and a livelihood rather than an abstract mandate to represent all Pashtuns. Protection and genuine ambition coexist. Specific household, occupation, ownership, training and route into the alliance remain open. This accepts the external-aim foundation (brief 2/6); it does not settle her flaw, moral boundary or episode choices. Earlier exploratory status is superseded only to this extent.

Next proposal, not accepted: a mother who is an active, capable participant in the family livelihood could be an intimate anchor. Mother understands her daughter's ability and ambition, yet their interests can diverge: daughter seeks leverage over outside decisions while mother must sustain day-to-day relationships at home. Neither is automatically conservative, oppressed, dependent or correct. An illustrative disagreement could concern an arrangement that secures the household economically but makes neighbors distrust it. No occupation, contract mechanism, father absence or family structure is established by this proposal. Ask whether the Director prefers a mother-daughter anchor or another relationship.
