### Narrative and voice proposal

Recommend the British officer as an in-world narrator: professionally assured, human, not a theatrical villain or omniscient historian. His classification of people can be complicated by the agency we see. Preserve his gradual moral arc; do not start him as fully disillusioned without a creative decision.

Do not compress a general martial-races lecture, a Pashtun lecture and the escape story into18seconds. Prefer one short verified quotation plus sparse original officer framing. Prepare at most two meaningfully different short treatments with one recommendation, timed against the existing edit. Preserve breath and sound space around recognition, the spectacular turn and determined continuation. Exact wording and voice remain unselected.

Archival quotation rules:

- Verify author, work, date, edition and page against a primary scan, checking OCR and surrounding context. Preserve meaningful qualifications and mark edits/ellipses.
- Separate fictional officer dialogue from words actually quoted; do not falsely attribute our writing to a historical officer.
- Explain the colonial viewpoint rather than endorsing its racial claims. A statement about male recruits or “Pathans” does not authenticate all Pashtun women or this fictional protagonist.
- Verify chronology and relevance within the reimagined world. Record anachronism if intentionally used; do not silently misdate evidence.
- Track rights separately for text, scan, font and recording. A public-domain text does not automatically clear every reproduction or performance.
- Use readable designed journal typography, with subtle age at the margins. A designed facsimile is not an authentic historical page; record that distinction. Do not fabricate a source scan.

Propose voice character and pronunciation needs separately from a provider or synthetic identity. Do not imitate a living actor without permission. Screen candidate voices in context only through already authorized resources; obtain any required paid-call approval first.
