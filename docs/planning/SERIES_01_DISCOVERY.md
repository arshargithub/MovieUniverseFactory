# Series 01 — Director discovery and release brief

Date: 2026-09-15. Status: **initial Director premise captured; detailed canon and release scope not frozen**. Working ID `series_01` is an organizational placeholder, not a title. This brief feeds [the overall roadmap](MOVIE_FACTORY_ROADMAP.md).

## Director premise — 2026-09-15

Source: Director's message in “AI Movie Factory - Design Work” on this date. The following are creative requirements supplied by the Director, not independently established historical facts.

- Setting: approximately 150 years ago, in northern British India and the adjacent Afghan frontier, broadly the region of modern Pakistan/Afghanistan. Exact date, jurisdictions, base and mission locations remain open.
- Core team: a Jatt Sikh protagonist, a Gurkha protagonist, and a female protagonist whose background is not yet chosen; Pashtun identity is one possibility, not an approved fact.
- They carry out missions for a British army officer while operating outside the official system. The officer's rank, institutional position, authority and relationship to the team remain to be developed.
- The three protagonists come from warrior/martial pedigrees. Treat this as family, community, training and lived history—not proof of inherited racial ability.
- Audience goal: bring subcontinental history and British-rule stories to a mainstream North American audience without requiring specialist knowledge or presenting the work as narrowly niche. The Director used “without it feeling too ethnic”; the proposed creative implementation is accessible stakes and storytelling while retaining cultural specificity, not erasing identity.
- Central theme: the colonial notion of “martial races,” the problematic category of race itself, and the tension between celebrated martial qualities and imperial expectations of useful, dependable service. The Director wants internal conflict in all three protagonists and the British officer.
- Possible title: **The Martials**. Working candidate only; no title selection or clearance performed.

The Director's thematic hypothesis includes British preference for recruits perceived as mercenary and less likely to revolt. Preserve this as a question to research and dramatize, not a narrator-endorsed statement that particular peoples were inherently mercenary, loyal, or disloyal. Distinguish colonial classifications and recruitment calculations from each person's actual motives.

## Development interpretation — proposed, not canon

### Subsequent Director decisions and family anchors

- Religious backgrounds: Sikh protagonist, Hindu Gurkha protagonist, and a strong Muslim female protagonist. Pashtun identity remains tentative; do not treat “Jatt,” “Gurkha,” and “Pashtun” as three religions or assume community labels alone determine belief/practice.
- Relationship direction: founding event(s) with a central role for the British officer, followed by costly mutual support and trust earned over time. Conflict within the group should be explored and overcome without requiring identical beliefs.
- Opening structure agreed: the team already works together, but trust is incomplete. Episode one demonstrates competence, chemistry and friction, then an order/discovery exposes a divide. Early episodes reveal shared history naturally; later consequential origin details change our understanding. Develop the founding mission privately before scripting; do not automatically open with it.
- **Trailer development is paused at the Director's request.** The Director has their own ideas to share later. Do not propose a trailer treatment or force a release-length decision during current character work.
- The Sikh protagonist has a **father and younger sister**. Their names, ages, personalities and histories are not yet chosen. The mother's circumstances and any other relatives are unspecified; do not infer death or absence from not being mentioned.
- Father/son direction accepted by the Director: the father helped steer his son into British service; their bond is not defined by overt condemnation for serving the conqueror. Shared responsibility, affection, pride and accommodation provide the basis for more nuanced tensions. Specific ages and military history remain proposals.
- The Sikh protagonist is personally close to the Director, but need not be the audience's favourite or the sole point-of-view character. The Director wants to explore pressure to prove martial masculinity. Treat contemporary lived observations as a creative lens, not evidence that all historical Jatt Sikh men thought alike.
- [Historical and family development notes](SERIES_01_SIKH_FAMILY_OPTIONS.md) distinguish explicit Director acceptances from the remaining proposals; do not treat the whole document as approved canon.
- Planning exchange 3: Director accepts the Sikh protagonist's central motivation: **agency is what he wants; influence is what the officer offers him.** The example missions and his proposed controlling behaviour remain unapproved; see the acceptance scope in the family development notes.
- Planning exchange 4: Director accepts that the officer sincerely believes he is empowering the protagonist, while expecting that freedom to operate within his own judgment. The officer is **not consciously transactional** in this relationship. Genuine respect/affection and unequal authority coexist; this does not establish his full beliefs, history or specific actions.
- Planning exchange 5: the Sikh protagonist is warm and funny, even goofy, especially with his sister. She is the person he is most comfortable with. His different manner with his father is clarified in exchange 6.
- Planning exchange 6: with his sister he need not be impressive; with his father there is affection but more self-consciousness and a desire to be seen as capable. Do not infer fear, coldness or lack of humour in the father. Specific scene examples remain illustrative, and the protagonist's proposed difficulty admitting uncertainty is not yet accepted.

The story can combine an accessible mission-adventure structure with a continuing question: **What does honour require when the power that rewards your courage also depends on your obedience?** Each protagonist needs a distinct reason to work with the officer, something the arrangement provides, and a line they will not cross. The officer needs convictions and interests of his own, not simply the role of enlightened rescuer or one-dimensional villain. The female lead needs an independently grounded role, training and agency; her ethnicity alone should not supply her function.

An illustrative dramatic engine is a mission whose officially stated purpose conflicts with what the team discovers on the ground. This is a development proposal, not an approved first episode. Character choices should reveal the theme before exposition names it.

## Historical anchors and research boundaries

Roughly 150 years before 2026 suggests the 1870s; **1870s–1880s** is a candidate research window, not a locked setting. The Second Afghan War of 1878–80 and frontier politics offer relevant context. Do not describe Afghanistan as simply part of British India, project modern national borders backwards, or use the later North-West Frontier Province administrative name prematurely. The [National Army Museum frontier overview](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/north-west-frontier-india) dates the province to 1901 and the Durand boundary agreement to 1893. Its [Gurkha overview](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/gurkhas) documents participation in the Second Afghan War.

Before choosing the exact year, research the evolution of martial-race recruitment ideology and contemporary vocabulary, relevant regiments and irregular/political arrangements, Sikh and Nepali recruitment contexts, Pashtun local politics, and plausible women's roles and training. “Unofficial missions” is fiction needing a credible institutional mechanism, not yet a verified historical unit. Existing irregular formations are research leads, not automatic permission to invent a modern special-operations structure in period costume.

These museum sources provide preliminary chronology, not a sufficient or neutral account of colonial recruitment and regional experience. Broaden the historical basis to scholarship and South Asian/Afghan/Nepali perspectives, including women's histories; distinguish contemporary colonial claims from critical interpretation. No comprehensive historical validation has been performed.
